SIP failure reference

Short pages for the moment you already have the trace open and want to know what the thing you are looking at actually means. No vendor marketing, no "contact your provider" — the mechanism, the causes that are common in the field, and the specific things to look for in the capture.

  • 488 Not Acceptable Here

    Almost always SDP: no codec in common, an SRTP/AVP mismatch, or a T.38 re-INVITE the far end will not take.

  • 408 Request Timeout

    Usually generated by your own stack when Timer B expired at 32 seconds. The diagnosis is in the packets that never arrived.

  • 486 Busy Here

    Sometimes a busy phone. Often call admission control, a full hunt group, or Q.850 cause 17 arriving from the PSTN.

  • One-way audio

    Signalling succeeded, media did not. Private addresses in c=, latching that never happened, or media anchored on only one leg.

  • Retransmissions and storms

    What the RFC 3261 timers do, how to read a retransmission ladder, and why 200 OK retransmits are the most diagnostic pattern in SIP.

Reading any SIP failure

Four habits that shorten most investigations, whatever the code turns out to be.

The status code says who gave up, not why

A final response is a verdict, and the evidence is upstream of it. The useful message is nearly always the last request before the failure — the offer that could not be answered, the INVITE nobody replied to, the re-INVITE that changed something. Read backwards from the code, not forwards.

Work out which box actually generated the response

A 4xx that your SBC produced locally and a 4xx it relayed from the carrier are completely different faults with the same three digits. Compare the Via stack on the response with the one on the request, look at whether the response carries the far end's User-Agent or Server header, and check whether the timing is plausible for a round trip to the network at all. A rejection that comes back in two milliseconds did not travel anywhere.

Absence of packets is evidence

Silence has a shape. Six retransmissions and nothing back means the far end never saw the request or could not answer it; a prompt 100 Trying followed by three minutes of nothing means it did see it and got stuck. Both are hard facts, and neither one is visible if you only read the messages that exist.

Signalling success is not media success

200 OK and ACK prove the two ends agreed on a description of the media. They prove nothing about whether a single RTP packet made it. When the complaint is audio, the SIP is context and the media is the case.
