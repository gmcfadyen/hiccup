Microsoft Teams Direct Routing: what an SBC has to get right

Short version: Direct Routing connects your own SBC to Microsoft's phone-system proxies so Teams users can make and take PSTN calls through it. Microsoft is strict about a short list of things on every SBC: a working TLS session with the right certificate, an answered OPTIONS keep-alive, the SBC's FQDN in Contact, SRTP towards Teams, and numbers in E.164. Nearly every Direct Routing fault is one of them, and each leaves a distinct mark in a capture.

The shape of the connection

The SBC sits between two worlds: a carrier or PBX on one side, and the Microsoft signalling proxies (sip.pstnhub.microsoft.com, plus the sip2 and sip3 names) on the other. Signalling to Microsoft is SIP over TLS, port 5061, with certificates checked in both directions. Media is SRTP. The SBC terminates all of that and talks plain RTP (or whatever the carrier wants) on its other leg, which is why a Direct Routing capture is always a back-to-back story: two legs that must each be read on their own terms.

Reading a Direct Routing capture

  1. Did TLS come up? If the capture is raw, look at the handshake first: SNI, the certificate's names and dates, and any alert. A certificate that does not cover the name asked for is rejected by Microsoft whatever else is right.
  2. Is the SBC answering OPTIONS? The Microsoft proxy polls it constantly; the pattern of answers tells you whether the SBC is in service.
  3. Compare the two legs. The Teams side must carry E.164 numbers, the SBC FQDN in Contact and an SRTP offer; the carrier side usually will not. Differences that the SBC is meant to fix are your configuration to-do list.
  4. Read the Microsoft response. A 403 is usually tenant definition or certificate name, a 404 is usually a number or voice route, a 488 is media security or codecs.

What hiccup does with it

hiccup recognises Direct Routing from the Microsoft proxy names, the PSTNHub User-Agent and the X-MS headers (or from the SNI and certificate names when the SIP is encrypted), says plainly that this is what it thinks you are trying to do, and then reports the checks above with fixes for the Teams side (the PowerShell cmdlets) and for AudioCodes and Oracle/Acme SBCs. The configuration blocks are drafts to check against your vendor's guide. Microsoft changes details such as signalling address ranges and trusted CAs over time, so confirm those against its current Direct Routing documentation.
